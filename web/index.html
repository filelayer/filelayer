The file layer for SaaS applications.

Per-file permissions, links you can take back, and a record of every allowed and denied access, for the files your users upload. A library in your own process, on your own Postgres and the S3 or R2 bucket you already own.

Keep your storage. You are adding a dependency, not a vendor.

What your agent would write by hand

Ask a coding agent for private uploads, sharing you can take back and an access log, and it writes them into your application. We had agents do exactly that, four ways, each following its platform's own documentation, and counted what they wrote.

  • Lines of application and infrastructure code, wired as in productionabout 2.9 times as many
  • Kinds of security-sensitive decision left to whoever maintains the codeabout 3.1 times as many

The two alternatives that ran took 794 and 569 lines against Filelayer's 233, and 25 and 24 kinds of security-sensitive decision against its 8: places where a wrong choice leaks data and nothing reports it. The hand-written versions can be made safe. Once revised against the attacks, raw S3 and Supabase defended all 21 shared attacks, as Filelayer does. The difference is how much you write, and how much you then have to keep right. The method, the results, and where each alternative is better →

In production, on your Postgres and your bucket

One file model, from simple to complex

A public avatar and a board deck under legal hold are the same row, authorized by the same function, on the same table. There is no second code path for the simple case, and no migration when the simple case grows up.

How it fits

You tell Filelayer who the caller is. It decides what they may do, serves the bytes with the right headers, and writes the audit event.

Your bucket keeps the bytes. Your Postgres keeps the metadata, the grants and the audit chain. Filelayer owns neither. It owns the model between them. Nothing moves, and nothing about your storage bill changes.

Node ≥ 22 and a Postgres you already run. The package installs zero runtime dependencies.

Your application
Knows who the caller is. Passes that identity down.
Filelayer
Ownership, organizations, access, sharing, expiry, revocation, lifecycle, audit, byte delivery.
Your S3 / R2 bucket  ·  your Postgres
Your account, your bill, your data.

It is authorization middleware, not row-level security: a client that queries your Postgres directly bypasses it. Where that boundary is, exactly →

Readable by machines, not just by people

Every one of these ships inside the npm tarball.

  • llms.txt: the integration map, ordered by what an implementer has to get right first.
  • An MCP server: filelayer-mcp, a stdio server you run yourself. One process acts for one user, fixed at start. Nothing is hosted.
  • The Agent Skill, for the agent writing the integration, and a CLI (filelayer doctor, schema status, usage-report) for checking a deployment.
  • errors.json, every error code with what it means and what to do, and OpenAPI 3.1 for the two HTTP routes it serves.
  • The tests: 772 across 169 suites travel with the package, so you can run them against what you installed.
