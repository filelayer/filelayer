One file feature, built five ways

A B2B document workspace, built five times: once with Filelayer and four times the way each platform's own documentation recommends. Then the same scripts count what each one asks the developer to write and to get right, and the same 26 attacks are run against every version that can run.

The workspace is the same in all five: organizations and roles, upload, per-role access, share links with an expiry, a password and a download cap, revocation of a link already handed out, and an audit trail.

Everything here is reproducible from a fresh clone with one command, and every number comes from data in the repository that you can change to get your own.

git clone https://github.com/filelayer/filelayer.git
cd filelayer
node benchmark/run.mjs

It needs Node 22.18 or later, no account, no credential and no environment variable. The network is used for npm and nothing else.

Who built them. We did, in September 2026. Each alternative was written by a separate AI agent, told to follow its platform's documented best practice and to argue for that platform. Each one's directory has a REPORT.md with its sources, what it could not implement, and a section called "Where this approach is genuinely good". The brief they were built from and the agents' prompts were not kept; benchmark/BRIEF.md says so, quotes what survives of the brief in their reports, and publishes the prompts that do exist. The Filelayer version is the examples/vault/server.ts that ships in the npm package, and a second copy of it wired as in production is counted beside it. The attack suite was written by the same team that builds Filelayer. That is a conflict of interest, and the section on the attacks says what we did about it.

Every alternative had one revision round. The library was fixed each time the attacks found a defect in it; the alternatives were frozen after one build. So on 13 November each executed alternative got one round against the attacks, at most 40 changed lines, done the way someone who knows the platform would do it, with the diff published in benchmark/revisions/. Raw S3 changed 39 lines, Supabase 8. Every result below is for the revised code. Convex and Vercel Blob have never run, so their code was not changed; their counts assume they were built the way their platforms document.

Security-sensitive decisions

A decision here is a point where the developer can choose an option that leaks or loses data and nothing reports it: no error, no failing test, no warning. Counted as kinds, and as the places in the code where each must be got right.

Over the two alternatives that ran, the median is 3.1x the kinds and 5.3x the places.

One rule for all five. A decision enforced in one helper is one place, however many call sites use the helper. A decision is dropped when the platform's own linter flags the wrong choice, or when the safe choice is the platform's default and leaving it takes a deliberate act: then the wrong choice is not silent. That rule removed two decisions from Filelayer (a private bucket and bucket versioning, both defaults), the same defaults wherever an alternative counted them, the two Supabase decisions its database linter flags, and a repeated count wherever one helper carries the decision. Filelayer's count also gained three decisions its own example gets wrong: the routes that create organizations and actors take no identity, the new organization's owner is read from the request body, and the request body has no size limit. Every change is recorded in decisions.json on the decision itself, with its reason. One Filelayer decision is listed as arguable; counting it gives Filelayer 9 kinds.

Two decisions that the first count in September held against Filelayer are no longer in it, because the library fixed them in 0.3.0: the audit chain could fork under concurrent writers, and a download cap was not charged on direct reads. Both are listed in decisions.json under removedSinceGate1, with the test that now holds each.

The Convex and Vercel Blob counts come from their authors' own lists only. The attack suite added decisions to both executed alternatives that their authors had missed, and it cannot do that for code that does not run, so those two counts are probably low. That works against Filelayer and is left as it is.

The 26 attacks

Written once, in benchmark/adversarial/attacks.mjs. They run over HTTP against Filelayer and raw S3, and as direct calls into the application's functions for Supabase, whose platform is emulated.

The comparison uses only the 18 attacks that every executed implementation can express. None of the three was breached on them.

ImplementationDefendedPartialBreach
Filelayer 0.35.218 of 1800
Raw S318 of 1800
Supabase, as built16 of 1820

Before the revision round, neither raw S3 nor Supabase recorded a refused cross-tenant read, or probes at documents that do not exist, in the application's own audit trail. Nothing was disclosed, and gateway or load balancer logs may have held them. A few lines closed it in both; the diffs are in benchmark/revisions/.

Supabase's two partials are one design choice, labelled "as built": its share gateway redirects to a signed URL valid for 30 seconds instead of streaming the bytes. So a revoked link keeps working for up to 30 seconds, and the audit records the issuing of a URL rather than a delivery. An Edge Function that streams the bytes would close both, at the cost of egress through the function; it was not built in the revision round, so these two results describe this implementation, not Supabase.

The other 8 attacks are reported apart and never added into that comparison. Most test a feature only some implementations have, such as per-file visibility inside a tenant, delegation or share listing; the rest depend on whether the application serves the bytes itself. Filelayer defends all 8. Raw S3 can express 4 of them and defends 3. On the fourth, HTML uploaded and opened on the signed-in read path, its presigned URL asks S3 to serve the file as an attachment, which real S3 does and the local store here does not implement: it is reported as "pass, not executed", not as a pass. Supabase can express none. "Cannot express" is never counted as a pass. One of the 8, escalation through delegation, is attempted as a direct SQL insert of a child grant, because the HTTP surface only creates read grants.

Why a perfect score is not the headline. A suite that the implementation it was written alongside passes in full is exactly the suite a reader should distrust. So it is also run against an earlier version of Filelayer's own example, from before its delivery and authorization were rebuilt, on the same library. That version is breached 3 times: a forged but well-formed caller id treated as real, among the 18, and among the other 8, a revoked download replayable from a cache and user-uploaded HTML rendered on the authenticated read path. The suite finds real defects in Filelayer's own code, which is the best evidence we have that it is not shaped around it. It is still our suite. Independent attacks are welcome, as an issue or a pull request.

From install to a stored file

From npm install to the first stored file, using the program printed in the quickstart that ships in the package: 4 lines of application code, no step that needs a person, and no account, credential or dashboard. On the machine that generated these results it took 4.6 seconds; seconds depend on the machine and the network.

Where the alternatives are better

Each alternative's REPORT.md has this section in full. The short version:

  • Supabase fails closed, and its rules cannot be bypassed by new code. No policy means no access, every rule is in one declarative file you can query and show a compliance auditor, and the policies apply to every path into the database, a psql session included. Filelayer is authorization middleware: it decides for calls made through it, and a direct SELECT against its tables is not checked by anything. If your team already runs on Supabase, it is one vendor instead of two.
  • Presigned URLs are the right answer when you do not need to take a link back. Bytes never touch your server, so there is no body limit, no memory pressure and no bandwidth bill on your app tier. For a 5 GB file it is the only sane option. Filelayer's default serves bytes through your process, because that is how revocation works on the next request, and that means no CDN on the private path.
  • Vercel Blob's private store makes immediate revocation the default. A private blob has no fetchable URL, so every byte goes through your code. Its credentials rotate on their own, and there is far less infrastructure to configure.
  • For a public, cacheable file, such as an avatar on a recipe site, a bucket and a CDN are the better tool. Filelayer can serve public files, but its value is in the private ones.

What this does not measure

  • Load, or anyone's production. Filelayer has no known production deployment.
  • How long each implementation took to build, or would take a person.
  • Two of the five alternatives, Convex and Vercel Blob, which were neither executed nor compiled.
  • Anything an independent security reviewer would: there has been no independent security review of Filelayer.

Disagreeing with a number

Every number comes from data in benchmark/. Remove a row from decisions.json, or change a rule in loc.manifest.json, run node benchmark/run.mjs again and you have your own figure. If you think an alternative was built badly, its directory says what it followed and where; an issue that shows the better version is the most useful thing you can send us.
